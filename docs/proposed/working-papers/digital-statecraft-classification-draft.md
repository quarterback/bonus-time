# The Work Was Always Statecraft: Classification, Evidence, and the Practice Layer

**Ron Bronson**
Taubman College of Architecture and Urban Planning, University of Michigan
A response and extension to Engin et al., "Governing Well in the Algorithmic Age: The Foundations of Digital Statecraft" (arXiv:2607.18483)

---

## Abstract

Engin et al. (2026) propose digital statecraft as the organising concept for effective and legitimate public governance in the algorithmic age. This paper accepts the concept and argues that its most immediate contribution is retroactive: it correctly classifies, for the first time, a category of work that has existed for decades and has been misfiled the entire time. National digital transformation was housed under the chief information officer and inherited that office's institutional position, a cost center governed by procurement, staffed through vendors, and structurally unconcerned with the impact layer, and the persistent failure of governments to deploy, scale, and ship is a consequence of that filing decision rather than of any deficit in talent or technique. The argument is an instance of established theory: professions are jurisdictional claims over task areas (Abbott, 1988), classifications are infrastructure that governs invisibly (Bowker & Star, 1999), and digital statecraft is a jurisdictional claim that reassigns the work from information technology to statecraft, whose objects are mandate, decision rights, permissions, rules, and scale. Three extensions follow. First, the digital service units founded between 2011 and 2018 constitute the field's first generation of institutions and its natural experiment, demonstrating both that the work requires a statecraft position and what misclassification costs. Second, the field's most conspicuous void is multilateral: a dense apparatus of standards, indices, funds, and specifications now exists, but no shared professional formation or durable practice infrastructure has emerged beneath it. Third, the founding paper's ability requirement is a digitally specific formulation of state capacity, connecting digital statecraft to four decades of capability literature and locating Public Mechanics as one downstream discipline through which that capacity is exercised. The paper answers two of the founding paper's six commissions along the way.

---

## 1. The founding paper's real contribution

The founding paper grounds digital statecraft in two requirements held in tension: ability, the practical power to use, understand, inspect, contest, enforce, and adapt; and legitimacy, the basis on which authority can be justified and held to account. Ability without legitimacy is authoritarian efficiency; legitimacy without ability is institutional impotence. From that tension it derives ten principles, of which three carry the distinctive weight: authority must be traceable through unbroken chains of authorisation or responsibility is performed rather than borne; legitimacy is temporal, because decisions foreclose futures and harms accumulate across individually contestable determinations; and a Non-Delegable Core of decisions cannot be delegated to systems regardless of capability, because a system cannot bear responsibility.

This paper accepts all of it and names what the framework does that its authors, building it conceptually, may not fully see. It resolves a classification error that practitioners have been working inside for thirty years. The choice of the word statecraft, over governance, over transformation, over modernization, is itself the contribution, because classification determines institutional position, and institutional position has been the binding constraint on this work since before the work had a name. Two of the founding paper's six commissions are answered in the course of the argument, and they should be named at the outset: the commission asking what international cooperation architecture the traceability condition requires receives its content in Section 4, a practice infrastructure rather than a further layer of standards; and the commission asking how the accumulated effects of algorithmic governance are made visible and correctable over time receives its answer in Section 5, as trace, aggregated, read by an institution mandated to read it. The paper's method should also be named: this is theory building on comparative institutional evidence, advancing the best supported reading of a record rather than a tested causal law, and Section 3 and the conclusion state the empirical program by which the theory should be disciplined.

The term itself has a lineage worth acknowledging, because the lineage clarifies what is new. The United States State Department briefly ran a "21st century statecraft" agenda around 2010, meaning technology in the service of diplomacy. Fourcade and Gordon (2020) used "statecraft in the digital age" for a critical account of what they call the dataist state, warning that it "may be less accountable than its predecessor," an argument the founding paper cites and absorbs. What no prior work appears to have argued, and this paper searched for it, is any of the following: that the digital service organizations collectively constituted a first generation of statecraft institutions; that classification under the CIO, rather than technology itself, explains repeated digital government failure; that the missing institutional layer of digital government is a shared practice infrastructure; or that the work belongs intellectually closer to international development than to enterprise IT. Those claims are this paper's, and they are stated here so they can be contested.

That the classification claim is not an intuition follows from two established bodies of theory. Abbott (1988) showed that professions are constituted not by bodies of knowledge but by jurisdictional claims over task areas, and that tasks change hands when a problem is successfully redefined as belonging to a different profession's jurisdiction; medicine took entire categories of human trouble from the clergy by redefining sin as illness. Bowker and Star (1999) showed that classifications are infrastructure: once made, they disappear from view and govern from inside their invisibility, distributing advantage and injury without anyone deciding to. Digital statecraft, read through Abbott, is a jurisdictional claim: it reassigns the task area of national digital transformation from the information technology profession, which has held it since the 1990s, to a new field whose objects are mandate, decision rights, permissions, rules, and scale. The remainder of this paper takes the claim seriously and asks what follows from it: what the evidence says (Section 3), what is missing internationally (Section 4), and how the field connects to practice (Section 5).

## 2. The misclassification and what it cost

When national governments first institutionalised responsibility for digital systems, they filed it under information technology. In the United States the filing has a statute and a date: the Clinger-Cohen Act of 1996 created the agency chief information officer and defined the office's object as the acquisition and management of information technology. The filing was reasonable at the time, and saying so strengthens rather than weakens the argument, because it establishes that the error is one of path dependence, not of original judgment. Clinger-Cohen was a procurement reform: it repealed the Brooks Act of 1965, decentralised IT acquisition authority from the General Services Administration to the agencies, and answered a decade of documented acquisition failures that the Government Accountability Office had placed on its high risk list, in the reform climate of the National Performance Review. In 1996 the systems in question were back office, the governing problem genuinely was purchasing discipline, and a purchasing office was the right answer. What happened next is precisely a Bowker and Star classification event. "To classify is human," their book opens, and classifications, once made, sink into infrastructure: nobody has examined this one in decades, and it has governed everything built since. The systems moved from the back office into the exercise of public authority, determining eligibility, benefits, and liberty, and the office responsible for them remained, constitutionally, a purchasing function: a cost center rather than a mission, governed by acquisition regulation rather than mandate, evaluated on uptime and spend rather than on what the systems did to the people subject to them.

Fountain (2001) supplied the theory of why the position is self perpetuating: institutions enact technology through their existing structures, so a capability filed under IT is absorbed into IT's routines, IT's vendors, and IT's silence about outcomes. DiMaggio and Powell (1983) supplied the theory of why every jurisdiction converged on the same arrangement: institutional isomorphism, the tendency of organizations facing uncertainty to copy legitimate forms regardless of fit, so that a CIO office everywhere asks what a CIO office does rather than what the situation requires (March & Olsen, 1989). Dunleavy and Margetts (2006, 2025) documented the result across three waves of digital era governance: the work grew in consequence while its institutional position stayed fixed, so that by the time algorithmic systems were determining eligibility, benefits, and liberty, the office responsible for them was still constitutionally a purchasing function. The practical texture of that position is familiar to anyone who has worked inside it. The CIO footprint is a mass of outside contracts. The operating skill it selects for is vendor management. Its relationship to the impact layer, what the deployed system does to discretion, burden, and rights, is not hostile but structurally unconcerned, because nothing in the position's mandate, funding, or evaluation reaches that layer.

The same classification failure is visible from the enforcement side, and the example matters because it involves no AI at all. Automated license plate reader networks and adjacent enforcement platforms have spread through thousands of jurisdictions as procurement events, equipment purchases inside the IT and public safety acquisition stream, with the result that surveillance architectures with obvious statecraft level consequences for rights and jurisdiction arrive without any statecraft level review, because the classification says they are equipment. Identity systems, payments rails, licensing platforms, and border systems follow the same path. The filing decision, not the technology, determines what review the deployment receives.

This is why deployment and scale have failed, and the diagnosis matters because every other diagnosis has been tried. The talent diagnosis produced fellowships and hiring authorities. The methods diagnosis produced agile procurement and human centered design mandates. The leadership diagnosis produced more CIOs, deputy CIOs, and chief digital officers, each inheriting the same position. What was never tried is reclassification, because until now there was no category to reclassify into. Statecraft is the category, and it is not a metaphor. The work concerns mandate: under what authority a government acts through systems. It concerns decision rights and permissions: who may decide what, at which layer, with what delegation, recorded how. It concerns rules as infrastructure and scale as a governing problem. Those are statecraft's objects, and the nearest existing professions are diplomacy, which manages state commitments across boundaries, and international development, which builds delivery capacity inside institutions the builder does not control. Neither enterprise IT nor the product practice of the technology industry, whose vocabulary the field still speaks and whose mindset remains its default register, has ever had those objects.

The category has a boundary, and stating it guards against concept stretching. Not everything a government does through computers is statecraft, any more than everything a government does abroad is diplomacy. Public administration is the running of the machinery; policy implementation is the translation of a particular enactment into practice; public management is the improvement of either. Digital statecraft is none of these. It is the layer at which a state constitutes its capacity to act through digital systems at all: the mandates, the authority structures, the standing institutions, the cross border commitments, and the professional formation. The test is whether the question at hand concerns the constitution of capacity or its exercise. Fixing a benefits portal is administration. Deciding under what authority, with what deployment structure, and with what accountability architecture a state builds and operates benefits determination as such, and negotiating how allied states do it interoperably, is statecraft. The boundary is a property of questions, not of projects, and a single project routinely crosses it midway: the team fixing the portal discovers that the eligibility rule it encodes contradicts the statute, and at that moment an administration task has surfaced a statecraft question. Under the current filing, that discovery has nowhere to go; the procurement function that owns the project has no jurisdiction over the mandate, so the question is resolved by whoever is standing there, silently, in code. What the classification correction supplies is not a cleaner line but a destination: an authority to which the discovered mandate question can be escalated and by which its answer is owned. The escalation discipline is what prevents the destination from becoming a catch all: the question travels upward and the project does not. When a construction crew discovers an unpermitted structural change, the question goes to the building authority and the crew keeps building to the answer; the escalation does not make carpentry a branch of law, and it does not transfer the site. Digital statecraft's jurisdiction is over mandate questions wherever they surface, not over every digital touchpoint that might surface one, and a statecraft office that begins absorbing projects rather than answering questions has recreated the CIO's empire at a higher altitude, which the boundary test exists to name. The exercise layer is where the delivery disciplines live, and this paper returns to it in Section 5.

One acknowledgment is owed here, because the capability literature this paper leans on makes it unavoidable: the filing is not only an error, and in some jurisdictions it is not an error at all. Andrews, Pritchett, and Woolcock are explicit that capability failures persist because they serve someone; a weak digital function is convenient for legislatures that prefer a state that cannot see itself too clearly, for vendors whose margins live in the gap, and for incumbents whose standing rests on controlling the ambiguity a capable system would remove. The CIO position is weak partly by design, and reclassification therefore faces headwinds that a purely cognitive account of the mistake would miss. This does not weaken the classification argument; it explains why the classification has survived thirty years of visible failure, and it means the correction will be resisted as a redistribution of power, because it is one.

It follows that reclassification is political engineering, not memo writing, and the record already shows what its politics look like. Every successful reclassification in the evidence of Section 3 travelled through a window in Kingdon's (1984) sense, a moment when crisis, political turnover, and a prepared alternative aligned: GDS was created inside a new coalition government's austerity program, with the Treasury's spend control as the price extracted for savings; Ukraine's ministry was created by an incoming administration elected on an anti bureaucratic mandate, and its platform was forged first by a pandemic and then by a war; India's UIDAI rode a new government's flagship commitment. None of them was achieved by persuading the incumbent classification of its error. Each was achieved by a political principal, at a window, installing the new position before resistance could organise. This resolves what might otherwise look like a tension between waiting for a window and building the field, because Kingdon's own finding is that windows are wasted on the unprepared: the window opens briefly, and what passes through it is whichever fully specified alternative is already standing at the door. Building the field, the category, the evidence base, the formation, the practice infrastructure, is not an alternative to the political strategy. It is the political strategy, executed during the years the window is closed. This paper is a piece of that preparation and claims to be nothing more; the engineering of any particular correction, jurisdiction by jurisdiction, is a program of work in its own right, and confusing having the analysis with having the mandate is the field's oldest mistake.

It is also worth saying plainly where the AI debate sits in this frame, since the founding paper's occasion is the algorithmic age, and the relation should be stated carefully rather than dismissively. The classification problem is old: national algorithmic determination systems arrive through the same IT procurement stream as the license plate readers, are reviewed by the same purchasing function, and inherit the same structural unconcern with the impact layer. In that sense the AI problem is the old problem at higher stakes, arriving faster. But this does not make the founding paper's ten principles redundant with reclassification, and the two claims operate at different layers. Reclassification determines which authority holds the work; the principles determine what that authority must be able to do and refuse once it holds it. The Non-Delegable Core, in particular, is genuinely new content for the category, a class of decision no capability can license delegating, and it only becomes enforceable at all once the work sits somewhere with the standing to enforce it, which no purchasing function has. Digital statecraft is the superset; the algorithmic age is what made the misfiling untenable, because acceleration turned a chronic condition acute; and the founding paper's principles are the content of the jurisdiction once claimed, not a substitute for claiming it.

## 3. The natural experiment the field already ran

The classification argument is testable, because governments ran the experiment. They had, in fact, run earlier versions of it. The United Kingdom's Central Computer and Telecommunications Agency centralised government computing expertise from the 1970s; the United States Office of Technology Assessment supplied Congress with standing technical capacity from 1972 until its abolition in 1995; national computing centers of the same era attempted the same concentration elsewhere. All were positioned as advisory or infrastructural functions rather than as instruments of state capacity, all were politically vulnerable for exactly that reason, and their fates, absorption, marginalisation, abolition, rehearse the pattern this section documents. What changed by 2011 was not the idea of centralised technical capacity but the location of the systems themselves: the internet had moved them from the back office to the front of the state, so that the interface of government and the delivery of government became the same artifact, and positional weakness that had previously cost efficiency began costing legitimacy. That is why the digital service units appeared when they did, and why this paper counts them as the first generation of digital statecraft institutions rather than the first attempt at government computing.

Between 2011 and 2018, that generation was founded: the Government Digital Service in the United Kingdom, 18F and the United States Digital Service, the Canadian Digital Service, Australia's Digital Transformation Agency, Singapore's GovTech, New Zealand's Service Innovation Lab, Denmark's Agency for Digital Government, and their peers. That they constitute one class rather than a scatter of local experiments is not this paper's assertion; it is the finding of the comparative research program that studied them as a class in real time. The annual State of Digital Transformation convenings at Harvard Kennedy School gathered the units from 2018 onward into a single frame, published comparative reports, and proposed a common maturity model across nine plus national teams (Eaves & McGuire, 2018; Eaves & Clement, 2020). The units assembled the right population, practitioners holding technical ability and public purpose together, and their multidisciplinary teams deployed into agencies were digital statecraft practiced before the field had a name.

The units differed on one decisive variable, institutional position, and the variable behaved exactly as the classification argument predicts. GDS was centrally funded by the Treasury and held spend controls over departmental technology expenditure: a statecraft position, mandate and money independent of the institutions it acted on, and departments came to it as an authority. 18F operated on cost recovery under the Economy Act, billing agencies through interagency agreements, its survival dependent on agencies choosing to buy; the Government Accountability Office (2016) documented that it had operated at a loss since inception and questioned whether transformation could be sold at cost. The deeper finding was positional. A unit that must sell its services to the institutions it exists to change is a vendor, and a vendor's scope is set by the buyer. Cost recovery placed the unit back inside the very procurement apparatus the work needed to fly above, and a unit in that position can be defunded by its clients one declined agreement at a time, or eliminated outright, as 18F was in March 2025. I write this as someone who spent nearly a decade inside 18F and led its design practice: the people were the right people, the craft was real, and the chassis was a policy choice.

The ceiling was called in real time, which is worth putting on the record because it converts this diagnosis from hindsight into a documented prediction. Surveying the units in 2018, Eaves concluded that they had produced tactical wins at the project level and almost nothing at the enterprise level, and that what came next was "a long slow battle over what the structure and shape of government will look like," progress on which he feared would be "infinitely more difficult and painful than improving services on a project by project basis" (Eaves, 2018). He located the constraint in what Moore (2013) calls capacity for authority, which is this paper's institutional position under another name. The prediction held.

Honesty about the success case is owed here, because the comparison is less tidy than the two paragraph version. GDS did not sustain its position; it held it. The spend controls were eroded over the following decade, the unit's mandate was progressively absorbed, and what remains sits inside a line department rather than at the center. The correct statement of the finding is therefore narrower and, on inspection, stronger: no unit anywhere achieved enterprise level results without a statecraft position, and no unit anywhere has kept a statecraft position without continuously re winning it, because position is not a grant but a standing political accomplishment, contested every budget cycle by exactly the interests the political economy paragraph named. Position is necessary. Nothing in the record shows it sufficient, and Section 3's second generation, below, confirms the same asymmetry from the other side.

The re winning requirement might seem to break the paper's Abbott foundation, as if the evidence showed a permanent campaign where the theory promised a settlable profession. It shows no such conflict, because Abbott promised no settlement. The System of Professions is explicitly a theory of perpetual contest: jurisdictional claims are never won finally, only held as temporary equilibria against active challengers, and medicine, the canonical winner, re fights its boundaries continuously against nursing, pharmacy, and now machine diagnosis. What distinguishes a profession from a mere faction in permanent warfare is not that its jurisdiction stops being contested but that it holds campaign infrastructure: formation, credential, a shared ethic, and a body of cases, which together lower the cost of every re winning until defense becomes maintenance. That is the precise institutional function of the practice infrastructure Section 4 calls for, and it is why the field's absence of formation is not a gap in its amenities but a gap in its ability to hold any position it wins. A profession is a permanent campaign that has built its logistics. Digital statecraft has so far fought every engagement as an improvisation.

Precision is also owed on what misclassification is fatal to. Not to governments, which ship harmful systems indefinitely and remain standing; the patient that dies is the work, the transformation itself, and the cost of the death is paid elsewhere. It is paid by the people subject to the systems, in the learning, compliance, and psychological burdens that administrative failure distributes downward (Herd & Moynihan, 2018), and it is paid regressively, because the impact layer that the misclassified office is structurally unconcerned with lands hardest on those least equipped to contest it: Robodebt's unlawful debts fell on welfare recipients and people with disabilities, not on the officials whose review had been evacuated. Misclassification is fatal to the work and distributively unjust in who absorbs the fatality, and the second half of that sentence is the reason the first half matters.

A second generation of evidence has since accumulated, and it runs in the confirming direction. The governments that filed the work at the statecraft level got statecraft results. Ukraine created a Ministry of Digital Transformation in 2019 and placed its minister at deputy prime minister rank, later first deputy prime minister: not an office inside the machinery but a seat at the cabinet's core, with its own budget and a mandate over every ministry's services. The result was Diia, a national services platform now used by over twenty million citizens, which scaled through a pandemic and then through a full scale war, absorbing wartime functions no CIO shop anywhere has ever been asked to carry. In January 2026 the minister who built it was appointed Minister of Defence, which is the classification argument concluded by a parliament: the state that positioned digital delivery as statecraft promoted its digital minister to the most consequential portfolio it has. The reassignment is also the harder test of the previous paragraph's point, and Ukraine passed it: the ministry continued under a successor drawn from the original reform team, which is the difference between a position and a person. A position that survives its founder has been institutionalised; one that does not was a personality with a budget. Taiwan drew the same conclusion in 2022, establishing digital affairs as a cabinet ministry rather than an office.

India is the boundary case, and it tests the argument from both sides. India did not file its identity stack under a CIO. The Unique Identification Authority of India was created as a mission with cabinet level sponsorship, later a statutory authority under its own act, led from outside the IT cadre, and the result scaled to over a billion enrolled residents and became the prototype the entire digital public infrastructure movement now generalises. As a test of the ability side, UIDAI confirms the position variable as decisively as any case available. But it also demonstrates why position is necessary and not sufficient, because what India constituted at statecraft level was capacity without the founding paper's second requirement: Aadhaar's legitimacy was contested all the way to the Supreme Court, which upheld the system while striking portions of its use, and the program remains the standing illustration that a statecraft position amplifies whatever accountability architecture it is given, including an inadequate one. The pattern across the second generation is therefore one finding stated three ways: position, not talent or technique, is the variable that determines whether the work can scale, and legitimacy is the variable that determines what the scaling is worth.

The obvious worry about elevation should be answered directly, because it is the founding paper's own first failure mode: does moving this work to a statecraft position not risk the technocratic leviathan? The record supports the opposite conclusion, and the inversion deserves stating plainly. The power in question already exists and is already being exercised; the current filing does not restrain it but conceals it. A determination system procured by a CIO office exercises exactly the authority over eligibility, benefits, and liberty that the same system would exercise under a ministry, with one difference: the CIO is invisible to the legislature, unconfirmed by it, questioned by no committee, and removable by no vote, while a minister is answerable in the chamber, dismissible by parliament, and attached to a government that faces elections. Ukraine's parliament confirmed its digital minister, extended his portfolio, and reassigned him; no legislature anywhere has done any of those things to the procurement chain that deployed a welfare algorithm. Misclassification is how the leviathan grows quietly. Reclassification does not create the power; it surfaces the power into the constitutional chain where the founding paper's legitimacy conditions can actually reach it, which is why elevation, done through statute and cabinet accountability rather than around them, is the democratic move and not the technocratic one.

Read as an experiment rather than a history, the record establishes two results the founding paper can now inherit as evidence: the work is statecraft shaped, because it succeeded precisely to the degree it held a statecraft position; and misclassification is fatal on a timescale of years, not decades.

The units' trajectory is also legible in the state capability literature, and the fit is close enough to constitute independent evidence. Andrews, Pritchett, and Woolcock (2017) identified the two traps that hold state capability down: isomorphic mimicry, adopting the forms of capability without the function, looking like a state rather than working like one; and premature load bearing, asking institutions to carry weight before capability exists. A digital strategy document, an AI principles page, and a digital service unit created by press release and starved by its funding model are isomorphic mimicry by the letter of the definition. A national algorithmic determination system deployed onto an agency that cannot inspect it is premature load bearing by the letter of the definition; Robodebt, the founding paper's own grounding case, is the type specimen. And the escape route the capability literature prescribes, locally defined problems, an authorizing environment, iteration, is nearly clause for clause a description of how the first generation units worked at their best. The units independently converged on the state capability playbook because the work is state capability work. Fukuyama (2013) defines state capacity as the ability to implement, distinct from the scope of state ambition; the founding paper's ability requirement is state capacity by another name. The connection matters because it is genuinely missing: the preprint's reference list contains neither Fukuyama nor Andrews, Pritchett, and Woolcock, nor any of the capability trap or implementation literature, reaching for Mann (1984) where forty years of closer empirical work was available. Supplying that connection grounds the framework's most abstract requirement in the most heavily evidenced concept in development economics, and it cuts both ways: the capability literature, in turn, has never had a name for the specifically digital form of the state capability problem, and digital statecraft is that name.

## 4. The multilateral void

The experiment also exposed what was never built, and this is the extension this paper most wants to press, because the founding paper derives an international cooperation requirement from its own traceability condition and leaves its content unspecified.

A dense multilateral apparatus for digital government now exists. The OECD runs peer reviews and a Digital Government Index. The World Bank runs GovTech maturity assessment and lending. UNDP and ITU run digital capacity programs. The Digital Public Goods Alliance certifies open source goods. GovStack, convened by ITU, GIZ, Estonia, and the Digital Impact Alliance, publishes building block specifications for identity, payments, registries, and information exchange, and runs sandboxes and training courses. The Digital Nations charter, signed as the D5 in 2014, convenes ministers annually. Every element of this apparatus is a standard, an index, a fund, a specification, or a short course. None produces practitioners. The pattern across the whole of it is that it professionalizes documents instead of people: interoperability gets a specification, the specification gets a sandbox, the sandbox gets a two week course capped at thirty officials, and the person who must make any of it work inside a ministry remains whoever happened to be standing there. There is no career grammar, no credential that travels between governments, no formation that a practitioner from one country and a practitioner from another would recognisably share. Diplomacy built its profession before it built most of its treaties. Digital government has built the treaties, the standards, and the summit circuit, and skipped the profession entirely.

The units of Section 3 were connected, but only as diaspora. The Canadian Digital Service was founded with 18F alumni in the room; GDS people seeded units on four continents; an informal common culture existed wherever they landed, and it evaporated wherever they left, because diaspora is not an institution.

The nearest existing exception proves the classification argument a second time. Estonia's e-Governance Academy, founded in 2002 by the Estonian government with UNDP and the Open Society Institute, has trained officials from well over a hundred countries and helped governments stand up interoperability infrastructure on the X-Road pattern. It is small, it is the only standing institution in the entire multilateral apparatus whose product is people rather than paper, and it is telling that it emerged from the one state whose digital capacity was built as a sovereignty project rather than an IT program. The obvious objection is that the exception has not replicated in two decades, which would matter if the transplantable element were the academy. It is not. The transplantable element is the sovereignty framing that produced the academy, and Ukraine is the replication: a state that classified digital capacity as an existential project generated, within years, both the capacity and the impulse to teach it. A fair follow up asks whether only existential threat can generate the framing, which would reduce the framework to a crisis response unavailable to stable democracies. The record says otherwise: crisis is one generator of the framing, not its only source. Singapore built GovTech at statecraft position under a national competitiveness framing, and Denmark's digitisation agency was built under a welfare state sustainability framing; neither had an enemy at the border. What the cases share is not threat but stakes: some account, security, survival, competitiveness, the fiscal viability of the state itself, under which digital capacity is a matter of national standing rather than office equipment. Any such account will do. The jurisdictions that lack one are not calm; they are unclassified, which is this paper's diagnosis restated. Academies are downstream of classification, which is the paper's thesis applied to its own best example.

Two honest caveats belong here, because the field should inherit them with open eyes. First, the governance of any practice infrastructure is itself a statecraft question, and an unsettled one: an infrastructure credentialed by donor governments risks conditionality, one run by multilateral agencies risks the document producing reflex this section describes, and one run by practitioners risks guild capture. This paper does not resolve the design and flags it as prior to any construction. Second, the intellectual adjacency to international development, which Section 2 claimed, imports development's pathologies along with its insight, and the DPI movement already shows the strain, with a national model generalised through donor backed campaigns and philanthropically funded intermediaries whose interests are not identical to the adopting states'. The adjacency is worth its cost for one reason: development is the only field that has spent forty years documenting its own failure modes honestly, mimicry, capture, conditionality, capability traps, which is forty years more honesty than enterprise IT has ever produced about itself. A field inherits its relatives' diseases either way; it is better to inherit from the relative who kept medical records.

The digital public infrastructure movement is the same lesson at larger scale, and it also demonstrates something the founding paper needs: that this field's object is one governing problem rather than a set of technologies, and that the demonstration owes nothing to AI. The DPI frame, prototyped in India's identity, payments, and data exchange stack and generalised through GovStack's building blocks and the fifty in five campaign, treats identity, payments, registries, licensing, and data exchange as a single layered whole of government architecture. It already moves between governments as an instrument of standing and alignment, India exporting its payments rails, Estonia exporting X-Road, which is statecraft in everything but staffing.

The founding paper's cooperation requirement is therefore not only a standards problem. It is a practice problem. Treaties, indices, technical specifications, and interoperability frameworks cannot by themselves produce the institutional judgment required to deploy them. What remains missing is a durable multilateral practice infrastructure through which governments can develop practitioners, circulate operational knowledge, and learn across jurisdictions without reducing the work to vendor led technical assistance. Abbott's theory predicts the consequence of leaving the gap open: a jurisdictional claim without formation and credential does not hold, because the field's knowledge cannot reproduce itself or travel. Until the practice infrastructure exists, digital statecraft will remain what digital government has been for fifteen years, a set of jurisdictions learning alone.

## 5. From statecraft to practice

A field classified this way requires a clear relationship between statecraft and delivery. At the statecraft level sit mandate, authority, institutional position, cross border commitments, and the accountability structures through which governments act digitally. At the delivery level sit the disciplines that make those commitments operational inside institutions.

Public Mechanics is one such discipline. It concerns how policy, money, authority, systems, and discretion combine into what a person actually receives from the state. Its relevance here is not as a competing theory of digital statecraft, but as evidence that the practice layer already exists in fragments. The problem has been that this work was housed beneath technology procurement, service design, modernization, or transformation rather than connected to a field with authority over the conditions under which digital systems govern. The same is true of the adjacent delivery disciplines, service design in government, delivery management, implementation practice: each developed real craft in isolation, each was filed under whatever office happened to host it, and none could appeal upward to a field that held the mandate question, because no such field existed. The classification correction changes what these disciplines are downstream of. One honesty is owed to the paper's own theoretical frame: naming a discipline is itself a jurisdictional claim, and Abbott predicts that adjacent practitioners will contest being placed downstream of anything. The claim made here is deliberately narrow to survive that contest. It is a claim about the layer relation, that delivery disciplines need a statecraft layer to escalate to, not a claim over any discipline's task area, and the difference between the two is exactly the difference, drawn in Section 2, between answering questions and absorbing projects. Jurisdictional contest is how professions form; the objection to the present arrangement is not that contest exists but that it has been conducted invisibly, inside procurement, for thirty years, with no forum in which the claims could be stated and opposed.

The founding paper's distinction between responsibility borne and responsibility performed provides one direct bridge between the two layers. Where the chain of authorisation is broken, the founding paper writes, "responsibility cannot be borne – only performed." What an institution says about a decision is testimony; what the decision mechanism records at the moment of action is trace. Testimony is reconstructable, negotiable, and produced for the audience that requests it. Trace is generated at the moment of deciding, by the mechanism of deciding. Digital statecraft establishes why authority must remain traceable. The practice layer determines how trace is produced, preserved, inspected, and used to correct accumulated effects over time. Robodebt, the founding paper's grounding case, is the demonstration of what happens when the two layers are severed: the progressive evacuation of human review was invisible in testimony and would have been visible in trace, and establishing it instead required a Royal Commission, years later, at national expense. The commission the founding paper leaves open, how accumulated effects of algorithmic governance are made visible and correctable over time, is answered across the two layers in one line: as trace, aggregated, by an institution mandated to read it.

A bootstrap problem must be acknowledged, because the paper's own diagnosis creates it: trace infrastructure has to be procured and built, and the builders currently work for the misclassified office whose structural unconcern is the problem. Two things break the circle. The first is that trace is already being produced under compulsion, just at the wrong time and price: courts and commissions extract it ex post, by subpoena and discovery, as Robodebt's Royal Commission did and as litigation over algorithmic determinations routinely does. The existence of ex post trace proves the systems can generate it; the correction moves the requirement from subpoena to specification. The second is that specification has a lever the diagnosis does not touch: the procurement contract itself. A trace requirement written into the solicitation binds the builder regardless of which office administers the purchase, because it converts an impact layer concern into a compliance term, which is the one language the misclassified office is structurally organised to enforce. The bootstrap is real, and it is escaped the way audit requirements have always been bootstrapped into unwilling industries, from the outside, in the contract, before the system exists to resist it.

The relationship is therefore hierarchical but reciprocal. Digital statecraft establishes the public mandate and legitimacy conditions. Delivery disciplines supply the operational methods by which those conditions become real, and return, in the other direction, the evidence of where the conditions fail, which is the only ground on which a field of practice can revise its foundations.

Four problems raised in the course of this argument are deliberately left open, and leaving them open is a method rather than an evasion; the founding paper closed with six commissions, and a field is built by the problems it names as much as by the ones it solves. First, the governance of the practice infrastructure: whether formation and credential are held by donor governments, multilateral bodies, or practitioners is itself a statecraft question, each option carries a named capture risk, and the design principle that balances them has not been found. Second, the development filter: the field needs a criterion for distinguishing healthy adjacency to international development from the importation of its pathologies, and the DPI movement, generalising a national model through donor funded intermediaries, is the live case on which such a criterion should be worked out. Third, the bootstrap's horizon: the procurement contract lever of this section is a bridge, not a settlement, because a compliance term administered by the misclassified office will be absorbed and gamed on the timescale that agile and human centered design were, and the durable answer is the reclassification itself, with the contract buying the years until a window opens. Fourth, the distributive theory: this paper has named who pays for misclassification and by what mechanism, but a full account of how a statecraft position should weigh the distribution of burden, not merely its traceability, remains to be written, and the delivery disciplines, which watch the distribution happen, are where its evidence will come from. These are offered in the founding paper's spirit, as the field's next commissions.

## 6. Conclusion

The founding paper names two failure modes, technocratic leviathan and platform sovereignty. There is a third, older and quieter, which this paper has argued the field was born inside: misclassification, the filing of statecraft under information technology, which for thirty years denied the work mandate, position, and a profession. The claim that talent, method, and leadership reform could not compensate for the filing is a counterfactual, and it should be held with the honesty counterfactuals require: the comparative record of Sections 2 and 3 makes it the best supported reading of the evidence, not a proof. The operative test is not a single decisive case but a covariation the record should display if the claim is right and should not if it is wrong: across units and across time, the degree of enterprise level change should track institutional position, hybrid and partial positions should show intermediate results, and the argument weakens with every counterexample in which the two come apart, a vendor positioned unit producing durable enterprise change, or a full statecraft position producing none for reasons position cannot explain. The record to date displays the covariation, including in its untidy cases: GDS's results eroded as its position eroded, which a talent or methods account cannot explain and a position account predicts. What the record establishes is that the filing itself was the operative decision, and that, as the jurisdiction and classification literatures predict, it governed from inside its invisibility for three decades. Digital statecraft is the first framework with the altitude to correct it. What the field inherits on arrival is more than its founders built: two earlier generations of centralised technical capacity whose abolitions rehearsed the pattern; a first generation of statecraft institutions already prototyped and demolished, with the experimental results recorded and the ceiling called in real time; a second generation, from Kyiv to Taipei to Delhi, confirming the position variable and demonstrating that position without legitimacy amplifies whatever it is given; a multilateral apparatus that has professionalized standards more successfully than practice; a state capacity literature that has described the field's failure modes under other names; and a set of delivery disciplines already operating beneath it without a shared institutional classification. The opportunity is not to invent a field. It is to coalesce one that has been operating without a name, and the name is now supplied.
