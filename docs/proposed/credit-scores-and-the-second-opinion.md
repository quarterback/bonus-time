# The end of the second opinion

**Format:** essay
**Inquiry:** Markets, platforms and value capture
**Worked out:** late July 2026, from a viral argument about FICO

## The argument

The pre 1989 lending world was a portfolio held by someone who lived in the market. A local bank kept the loan on its books, so the underwriter carried the loss, and local knowledge was cheap to gather. That is why a handshake could work. It also worked badly for anyone not near someone with authority.

Securitization broke both ends. Once a loan is sold, the originator's exposure ends at closing, and local knowledge doesn't survive the sale. The score exists to make loans comparable to strangers, which is a different job from predicting whether you'll pay. After interstate banking consolidation, one classification spreads everywhere at once, with no bank down the street to rule differently and nobody to argue with.

Two points the public argument missed:

- Defenders split into a moral camp (the score mirrors your habits) and a historical camp (scoring replaced discriminatory discretion). The historical defense is correct and it defeats the moral one: if scoring improved on discretion, the number never measured virtue in either era.
- The live harm is derived scores. A tenant with a 720 credit score gets a lower "renter score" computed by a method he can't see, and can't appeal it because the thing he would appeal, the 720, is fine.

Standardization removed one channel of discrimination and added another, because a model trained on lending history built under redlining reproduces the pattern without anyone deciding to.

Civil Economics, the derived score problem in the consumer agent score brief, and Finland's negative only credit register as a real counterfactual.
