# Sovereignty is lost in the contract

**Format:** working paper or long essay
**Inquiry:** Digital statecraft and public capability
**Worked out:** 1 November 2026

## The argument

Most of the software sovereignty debate treats sovereignty as a question about which country a vendor belongs to and whether a government can leave. It says almost nothing about whether an agency can see and change the rules its own system applies to people. A domestic vendor can lock a ministry out of its own eligibility logic as completely as a foreign one.

So sovereignty can be lost to a domestic contractor, and the place it is lost is the contract. A government that did not secure rights over source, data, audit, exit and the rules a system applies, at the moment it bought the system, has given away control of its own policy. Owning every layer of the stack is autarky and nobody can afford it. What a government can afford is to keep, in the contract, the ability to inspect, contest, adapt and leave.

This carries the European debate into a setting where nobody applies it: US subnational government, where the dependence runs on domestic vendors and nobody calls it a sovereignty problem.

## Literature to engage

- Julia Pohle and Thorsten Thiel, "Digital sovereignty," *Internet Policy Review* 9(4), 2020. The standard overview; sovereignty as a political claim more than a legal concept.
- Francesca Bria, Paul Timmers and Fausto Gernone, *EuroStack: A European Alternative for Digital Sovereignty*, Bertelsmann Stiftung, February 2025.
- Deirdre Mulligan and Kenneth Bamberger, "Procurement as policy," *Berkeley Technology Law Journal* 34, 2019. Closest existing argument: when systems embed policy, agencies must move from a procurement mindset to a policymaking one.
- Merve Hickok, *From Trustworthy AI Principles to Public Procurement Practices*, De Gruyter, 2024. A University of Michigan colleague.
- Matthias Stürmer and colleagues on measuring IT vendor lock in from Swiss procurement records (University of Bern, 2017).
- Per Persson and Johan Linåker on soft lock in to open source vendors (arXiv 2409.01118, 2024).

## Live cases

- European Commission Cloud Sovereignty Framework and its €180 million tender awarded April 2026.
- Schleswig-Holstein moving about 30,000 workstations off Microsoft.
- Denmark's digitization ministry dropping its Collabora pilot in February 2026 when an election was called, a sovereignty move with no protection losing to an election cycle.
