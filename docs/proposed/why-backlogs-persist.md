# Nobody is paid to clear the line

**Format:** essay
**Inquiry:** Digital statecraft and public capability
**Worked out:** October 2026

## The argument

Backlogs in public programs were never technically hard. They persist because nobody involved is paid to end them.

- Vendors are paid for multi year work, not a thirty day clear.
- Agency budgets and headcount justify themselves partly by the volume of pending work.
- Some people want access to services slowed, and a backlog does that without a vote.

The stance for the piece: say this openly, and clear the backlog anyway, because people are entitled to services they already pay for. Anyone who wants to cut or slow a program should do it in the legislature, in public.

The mechanism worth explaining: when a case file arrives with provenance showing every part has already been checked, the worker's job changes from inspecting every part to validating an inspected report. That removes the blocker and turns what remains into a question of accountability, which is where it belongs. Going from 40,000 pending cases to 10,000 is a win, and proving it once makes it repeatable.
