# The Kentucky Register: A Style Guide for Technical Career Biographies

**Status:** Working editorial guide · 22 October 2026  
**Purpose:** Describe professional responsibility, contribution, and accomplishment with the confidence and depth of an athletic-department staff biography, and the specificity of technical company writing.  
**Applies to:** Personal biographies, staff profiles, appointment announcements, speaker introductions, career histories, project credits, and longer professional records.  
**Scope:** Narrative language and structure only. This is not a visual design guide, résumé template, verification system, or sector-specific positioning strategy.

## Editorial proposition

A staff biography is **a portable record of the work someone has done**. The person's current title is the entry point, not the limit of the record.

The Kentucky model does three things unusually well:

1. It gives junior and specialist staff substantial biographies, even when the results belong to an entire program.
2. It ties the work of assistants, coordinators, analysts, and operations staff to the achievements of the teams they helped build or run.
3. It carries each person's record across institutions and promotions. Work done in an earlier, larger, or less visible assignment still counts.

The best commercial technology writing adds what the athletic register often leaves implicit: **the actual systems, products, decisions, and technical changes that someone made**.

The target style is therefore *a generous institutional record with technical precision*: someone held responsibility for an identifiable part of an organization, did consequential work, and contributed to outcomes worth remembering.

**Executive and delivery careers:** Section 13 translates design-, IT-, and communications-coded jobs into language of operational authority, technical leadership, and program delivery. Start there when an employment title understates the responsibility.

The narrative doesn't need to defend the importance of the field, explain someone's career philosophy, or apologize for the fact that work was collaborative.

### The important contrast

The Ohio State and Utah operations profiles competently enumerate duties. Kentucky's Love and Barham biographies do more: they turn duties into a record of what became possible and what the surrounding unit achieved. Georgia and Alabama use the same generous link between staff member and program performance. Oregon's promotions announcement supplies another mechanism: *an authority figure explicitly credits the people who made the program work*.

The convention is not “claim that an assistant single-handedly won the championship.” It is “their work is part of the professional history of the championship team, and the biography says so.”

### Four moves athletics uses repeatedly

**1. The tenure opener.** Start in the present: first season, third year, seventh season, returning appointment, promotion. A reader knows where the person stands.

**2. The unit possessive.** “Her unit,” “his players,” “the staff he helped assemble,” “under her direction.” A person's assigned part of the organization becomes the subject of a result sentence.

**3. Generous shared credit.** “Helped lead,” “was instrumental in,” “with her assistance,” “played a key role,” and sometimes “because of her efforts.” These phrases associate meaningful work with collective achievement. They do not demand solitary ownership.

**4. The career as an accumulated record.** Each stop receives a role, remit, achievement, and sometimes a first or a numerical reference point. The present appointment gains weight from the whole sequence.

### Three different types of technical credit

**The maker's record:** invented, prototyped, wrote, designed, shipped, maintained. The object is a feature, platform, API, tool, design system, or product.

**The owner's record:** selected the problem, made product decisions, chose the approach, led the release, measured adoption, maintained the result. This can belong to an individual contributor.

**The organizational builder's record:** staffed the team, created a capability, introduced a common operating practice, established a new function, and left something that continued to work. Stripe's Dublin hub is an especially strong example.

The intended register treats all three as accomplishments. It does not presume that only management produces leadership, or that only code produces technical value.

## 3. The linking grammar

These are the actual sentence moves to preserve. The left-hand column names the athletic construction; the right-hand column translates it into **invented technical examples**, not claims about an actual company or person.

| Athletic construction | Technical-register example |
| --- | --- |
| **Tenure opener:** enters her fourth season in the role | **“Maya Chen is in her fourth year as platform lead at Northstar.”** |
| **Arrival through last stop:** joins after three seasons at the prior program | **“Chen joined Northstar after three years on the developer tools team at Juniper.”** |
| **Principal names the standard:** leader explains what this hire brings | **“The CTO credited Chen with establishing the release discipline the company needed as its engineering organization grew.”** |
| **Unit possessive + metric + reference point:** her unit led its conference | **“Her team cut median deploy time from 40 minutes to six, its fastest release cycle to date.”** |
| **First:** introduced the institution's first program of its kind | **“She established the company's first production on-call rotation and incident review practice.”** |
| **Assistance tied to team result:** with her help, team ranked in the top 30 | **“With her assistance, the team finished its data migration two quarters ahead of schedule.”** |
| **Direct link to result:** because of his work, recruiting rose | **“His work on the onboarding system reduced the time required to provision an engineering workstation from three weeks to four days.”** |
| **Named initiative:** key contributor to a new operational hub | **“He was a key contributor to Atlas, a common deployment platform that replaced separate release procedures across six teams.”** |
| **Scope:** managed eight coaches across 19 sports and 400 athletes | **“She managed six engineers serving 14 product teams and more than 200 internal users.”** |
| **Involved in all aspects during a successful run** | **“He worked across architecture, reliability, and release engineering as the company grew from 40 to 300 engineers.”** |
| **Development of others:** under his guidance, players earned honors | **“Four engineers on her team were promoted to senior roles; two subsequently took charge of teams of their own.”** |
| **Promotion inside the organization:** advanced from student assistant | **“He joined as a support engineer in 2020, moved into product engineering in 2022, and was elevated to staff engineer in 2025.”** |
| **Support-role function list:** scouting, game planning, practice execution | **“Her work covered incident review, capacity planning, release management, vendor evaluation, and technical documentation.”** |
| **Earlier, larger assignment:** assistant presently, former department head | **“Before returning to hands-on engineering, he directed a 50-person software organization and oversaw its cloud migration.”** |
| **Nontraditional prior experience:** restaurant staff management included | **“Earlier, she managed warehouse operations across four sites, overseeing staffing, schedules, and inventory.”** |
| **Beyond the primary role:** influence beyond the field | **“Outside product development, she maintains an open-source library and mentors early-career engineers.”** |
| **Career table:** seasons, positions, program, milestone | **“At the end, list years, title, organization, and a signature technical or organizational result for each stop.”** |

These are *sentence constructions*, not canned marketing claims. Preserve the verbs, ownership, scale, and relationship to team success. Replace the example details with the actual record of whoever is being described.

### How to credit collective work

Use the strongest natural construction the person's role warrants:

- **Directed / led / oversaw:** managed the work, unit, decisions, or personnel.
- **Built / developed / designed / implemented:** personally made the technical or organizational thing.
- **Established / launched / introduced:** created a program, service, system, or capability and put it in use.
- **Helped lead / instrumental in / key contributor:** materially shaped a team outcome without sole control.
- **With their assistance / under their guidance / during their tenure:** places the professional visibly within a larger result.
- **Because of their efforts:** strong causal credit when the outcome can reasonably be linked to the person's contribution, as Kentucky does for Erynn Love.

**Do not impose a universal rule that support staff may only be associated with outcomes “during their tenure.”** That would erase Kentucky's most useful writing convention. Also do not transform a contribution into sole authorship; the available verbs already offer generous shared credit.

### Numbers without flattening the biography into a dashboard

Choose a few measurements that establish the scale or change:

- **People:** direct reports, team size, people trained or developed, teams supported.
- **Systems:** services, integrations, pipelines, codebases, clusters, deployments.
- **Reach:** active users, customers, transactions, regions, internal consumers.
- **Performance:** latency, failure rates, reliability, costs, release time, adoption.
- **Trajectory:** first, highest, fastest, earliest, from X to Y, across N years.
- **Continuity:** teams promoted, programs that remained operational, systems maintained after handoff.

A first can be as strong as a percentage. “Established the first shared release platform” is a substantial achievement if it is supported by the surrounding account of what the platform did.

## 4. How to assemble the biography

The register is expansive, chronological, and specific. It should read like a professional record assembled by someone who understands what the position required.

### Opening paragraph: current station and accumulated experience

Use a **tenure opener** or an arrival statement. Establish current work and the previous experience that qualifies the person to do it. Avoid beginning with beliefs, passions, or philosophy.

> **[Name] enters [their third year / a new appointment] as [role] at [organization], bringing [number] years of experience in [specific responsibility].**

### Second paragraph: current responsibilities and unit

Describe what the person is actually responsible for. The level of detail can be as specific as an operations coordinator's logistics or a principal engineer's architecture.

> **In the role, [Name] oversees [named team, systems, function], working with [adjacent roles] on [technical or operational responsibilities].**

### Third paragraph: an achievement at the current stop

Connect the responsibility to a result, first, launch, improvement, or period of strong team performance. Give the unit its possessive. Use visible individual credit.

> **In [year], [their team / unit] [measurable result]. [Name] [developed, guided, or led] [the relevant work], which [effect].**

### Subsequent paragraphs: earlier stations and their records

Move in reverse chronological order. **Every meaningful stop carries an achievement, not just a title and date.** Repeat the formula where material exists: responsibility + team result + contribution. Earlier leadership scope stays visible even if the current job is different.

### Closing: adjacent standing and chronology

Teaching, speaking, writing, professional communities, and other contributions may appear when they establish an additional record. Do not use them to replace the work history. Close with a compact career table when there are several stops.

### Placement of outside endorsements

An appointment can quote someone who hired or worked with the practitioner. The quote should identify a *specific capability or result*, not simply call the person brilliant or collaborative.

Useful: “She introduced the reliability practices that made the next three product launches possible.”

Weak: “She is an incredible teammate and a pleasure to work with.”

## 5. Model technical biographies (fictional examples)

These examples are invented entirely to show how the register sounds. All organizations, names, dates, statistics, and accomplishments in these examples are fictional.

### Model A — A specialist below executive rank

**Maya Chen — Staff Platform Engineer**

Maya Chen enters her fourth year at Northstar as a staff platform engineer, working across developer tooling and release reliability. She joined the company in 2023 after three years building internal services at Juniper.

At Northstar, Chen helped establish Atlas, the company's first shared deployment platform. The system consolidated release workflows across 14 product teams, replacing separate scripts and approval processes with a common path to production. Her team reduced median deployment time from 40 minutes to six and introduced automated rollback for the first time.

Chen joined Northstar as a senior engineer and was promoted to staff engineer in 2025. In that time she became a technical lead for the release platform, working with the infrastructure, product, and security teams on service ownership, incident response, and developer experience.

At Juniper, Chen worked on the data systems team during the company's expansion from three to eleven product lines. She was a key contributor to a Kafka migration that cut message-processing delays by more than half and subsequently helped onboard engineers across four teams to the new architecture.

### Model B — The operations specialist whose work enables wins

**Devon Park — Director of Engineering Operations**

Devon Park is in his second year as director of engineering operations at Halcyon, where he oversees release coordination, incident management, engineering onboarding, and vendor operations for the company's software organization.

Park came to Halcyon after serving as technical operations manager at Vale, where he was instrumental in introducing a common incident command process across seven product teams. With his assistance, the engineering organization reduced the time required to restore service after major incidents and completed two platform migrations without interruption to customer-facing releases.

He began at Vale as a support operations specialist in 2019 and steadily advanced through the organization, first managing the incident calendar and on-call logistics before assuming responsibility for engineering operations. In his final year, he coordinated the launch readiness process for four major products and supervised a team of five operations specialists.

Before entering software, Park spent six years managing regional logistics operations, including staffing, schedules, and vendor relationships across three facilities.

### Model C — Earlier authority remains fully visible

**Ari Patel — Principal Product Engineer**

Ari Patel joined Vesper in 2026 as a principal product engineer, bringing 15 years of experience building software products and leading engineering organizations.

Before joining Vesper, Patel was vice president of engineering at Meridian, where she directed a 60-person organization responsible for the company's web application, platform services, and developer experience. During her tenure, the group launched three new products, consolidated two engineering teams after an acquisition, and introduced a common design-and-code component library used across its product lines.

Previously, Patel was engineering director at Tern, where she established the mobile team's first common release process and managed the transition from a single application to a portfolio of products. Four engineers who worked under her leadership later became engineering managers.

Patel began her career as a frontend engineer and continues to contribute code, prototypes, and technical writing alongside her product work.

#### Sample career table

| Years | Role | Organization | Selected record |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2026– | Principal Product Engineer | Vesper | Product development and technical leadership |
| 2021–26 | VP Engineering | Meridian | Led 60-person organization; three product launches |
| 2017–21 | Engineering Director | Tern | Established common mobile release process |

### Why the models read this way

They do not open by explaining why the person is multidisciplinary. They begin with an appointment, move into the assigned work, credit the person within unit results, then show how the work accumulated across successive positions. Technical objects and measurements make the content specific; the athlete-bio grammar makes the professional record portable.

## 6. Working editorial rules

1. **Write generously about specialists and assistants.** A subordinate title deserves detailed credit when the work warrants it.
2. **Name the unit.** Team, function, system, program, product line, and operating scope are more informative than “cross-functional environment.”
3. **Give the collective result a place in the individual's record.** Use “his team,” “with her help,” “played a key role,” or “under their direction” naturally.
4. **Keep each stop alive.** An earlier assignment's responsibilities, scale, and achievements belong in the current biography.
5. **Give operations their own verbs.** Coordinated, staffed, implemented, maintained, trained, planned, introduced, managed, and oversaw are substantive when attached to concrete work.
6. **Name the thing built.** System, product, platform, API, model, workflow, release process, or organizational function.
7. **Use actual reference points.** First, time period, before/after, number of teams, user base, performance ranking, or result. Avoid naked claims that something was “transformative.”
8. **Document talent development.** Staff promotions, people trained, successors, team growth, and people who went on to lead their own work belong in the record.
9. **Let the biography be long enough.** The target is not a résumé summary. A major station may deserve multiple paragraphs.
10. **Allow prior scope to exceed current scope.** The person carries the record even when they move to a hands-on or supporting role.
11. **Use endorsements when they add authority.** An executive's specific account of why they selected or relied upon someone can establish stature.
12. **Do not turn every biography into a first-person philosophy.** Write what they were trusted to handle and what happened during the work.
13. **Avoid the résumé's endless job-title procession.** Each significant appointment should establish a responsibility or result.
14. **Avoid a restrictive attribution taxonomy.** The language of an actual appointment announcement is expansive, with multiple ways to credit team achievements fairly.
15. **Protect the professional record from euphemism.** If the person directed a function, say directed. If they made the product, say built. If they materially helped, say helped.

### Constructions to reduce

- “Passionate about…” when a tenure or accomplishment would do.
- “Worked across…” when the work can be named.
- “Supported various initiatives…” when a particular initiative or result is available.
- “Leveraged stakeholder engagement to drive impact…” when there is a straightforward account of decisions or implementation.
- “Thought leader / change agent / visionary…” when the person's record can demonstrate authority directly.
- Unexplained acronyms and internal project names that make the record legible only to former colleagues.
- Artificial paragraphs of qualifications after each accomplishment; do not make the biography rebut its own claims.

## 7. A short writing test

Compare the descriptions, all invented:

**Directory voice:** “Alina was a senior engineer at Southbank from 2021 to 2024. She has extensive experience in developer tooling, release processes, and team collaboration.”

**Athletic-register technical voice:** “Alina spent three years on Southbank's developer tooling team, where she helped establish the company's first shared release platform. Her team reduced deploy time from 50 minutes to eight while supporting more than 20 product teams. During her final year, she led the migration of the company's oldest services onto the new platform and mentored two engineers who were later promoted to senior roles.”

The second has **a tenure, an identifiable unit, a first, a before-and-after measure, a scope, and development credit**. It gives a practitioner below the executive level an actual professional record. It is not a claim that this person alone produced the company result.

## 9. Addendum: the Big Tech, startup, and SaaS registers (22 October 2026)

**This section extends Sections 1–8.** Keep the Kentucky career-credit grammar, the research across athletic departments, the technical examples, and the existing editorial rules. This is an additional vocabulary for explaining *scope, technical ownership, commercial consequences, and product adoption*. It does not replace the staff-biography structure with a performance review, startup pitch, or résumé.

### These are three different professional languages

| Convention | What the reader is meant to learn | Typical evidence | Blind spot to correct with another register |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Athletic-department biography** | What positions someone held, what they were responsible for, which teams they helped develop, and how those teams performed across their career. | Tenure, appointment, unit statistics, promotions, named programs, colleagues' accomplishments. | A team's success may be clearer than the specific tool, decision, or technical work behind it. |
| **Big Tech / senior engineering** | What a person owned, the breadth of their influence, the difficult decisions they made, and the organizational or technical change they produced. | Systems, architecture, cross-team scope, reliability, performance, staff development, launches, company-wide adoption. | The record is often kept in promotion packets and internal assessments rather than in a public career biography. |
| **Startup / SaaS / product engineering** | What a person took from idea to working product, who used it, what happened after launch, and how the product or business developed. | Prototypes, releases, activation, retention, user interviews, pricing experiments, performance, revenue where relevant. | Commercial metrics may obscure valuable organizational work and products that are not monetized. |

The editorial hybrid should answer **where the person did the work, what they owned, what they built, what changed, and how their contribution fits into the larger team's history**. Each of those can be a sentence; none needs to become a theory of leadership.

### Big Tech: breadth is a form of senior technical scope

The useful contrast is **deep scope** versus **broad scope**, not specialist versus generalist as a measure of seniority.

In a [Staff Engineer interview](https://staffeng.com/stories/michelle-bu/), Stripe payments-products technical lead Michelle Bu distinguishes specialists leading difficult work within a technical domain from broad-scoped engineers who take on ambiguous problems across several parts of the organization. She described working with the chief product officer on urgent initiatives and large cross-organizational projects, while supporting the teams that carried out the work. Her contribution was measured partly by the progress and direction of those teams.

[Slack staff data engineer Diana Pojar](https://staffeng.com/stories/diana-pojar/) makes a related distinction: breadth-oriented staff engineers can influence technical investment across an organization while also writing code, reviewing designs, mentoring, and responding to incidents. These descriptions establish technical standing *through the range and consequences of the work*, even without an executive title.

**Language to extract:**

- **Scope of influence:** company-wide, product-line-wide, across multiple teams, spanning a platform and its consumers.
- **Responsibility:** technical lead for, owned the architecture of, directed the migration, set the roadmap for, made the implementation call on.
- **Ambiguity:** took on an undefined problem, established a technical direction, resolved a cross-team dependency, redesigned a failing process.
- **Multiplying others' work:** enabled other teams to ship, developed staff engineers, established shared tooling, cleared the way for a product launch.

The Kentucky treatment makes these accomplishments part of the individual's *public career history*, rather than leaving them inside an internal promotion case.

### SaaS and product engineering: a product has a life after launch

[PostHog's Product Engineer Handbook](https://posthog.com/product-engineer/what-is-a-product-engineer) defines product engineering in terms of responsibility for the product's success, beyond writing code: talking with users, making product decisions, handling support, and choosing what to build. Its account gives a useful way to describe **zero-to-one work and what followed**.

| Term | What it should mean in a biography | A sentence construction (fictional) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Zero-to-one** | Took a problem or idea to its first functioning version. | “She took the scheduling product from initial concept to a working launch used by three pilot teams.” |
| **Shipped / launched** | Released something identifiable and usable. | “He shipped the first production version of the API in 2024.” |
| **Owned** | Held responsibility for decisions and results over time, not simply participated. | “She owned the product roadmap, implementation priorities, and release decisions.” |
| **Go-to-market (GTM)** | Defined how a product would reach, convert, or activate its intended users. | “He developed the early distribution strategy and brought the first 40 teams onto the product.” |
| **Activation / adoption** | People started accomplishing a real task using the product. | “Following launch, the tool was adopted by 12 engineering teams.” |
| **Retention** | People kept using it after the first encounter. | “The team's changes increased 30-day retention from [baseline] to [result].” |
| **Scaled** | Expanded the system, team, operation, or use while preserving performance. | “Under her direction, the team grew from four to 20 engineers and expanded the platform to five products.” |
| **Reliability / operational maturity** | Made a product or organization consistently work. | “He introduced on-call practices and reduced recovery time from [X] to [Y].” |

Revenue, pricing, and conversion are appropriate **when they were part of the assignment**. For free software, internal platforms, public resources, and nonprofit products, usage, repeat use, reuse, uptime, time saved, reach, and team independence may be better evidence. A metric is not a prerequisite for professional credit.

### Big Tech often records accomplishments internally; Kentucky publishes them

[Will Larson's guide to Staff Engineer promotion packets](https://staffeng.com/guides/promo-packets/) recommends recording major projects, organizational improvements, measurable results, mentorship, and work that enables other teams. This shows where much senior engineering accomplishment language is produced: inside promotion and performance processes.

The writing lesson is **to publish a readable career record that uses the precision of a promotion narrative and the generosity of a staff biography**. Do **not** transplant the packet's internal checklist, substantiation routine, or corporate evaluation language into the public bio. The earlier sections' attribution guidance remains unchanged.

### Linking grammar: adding technical ownership to Kentucky credit

| Kentucky-style career sentence | Technical/SaaS adaptation (all examples fictional) |
| --- | --- |
| “During her first two seasons, her unit recorded its strongest results in program history.” | “During her first two years, her platform team completed the company's fastest release cycle and expanded its services to nine product teams.” |
| “He was a key contributor to the program's first championship run.” | “He was a key contributor to the first production launch, building the data import service that enabled the customer rollout.” |
| “Under her guidance, three players earned all-conference honors.” | “Under her leadership, three engineers advanced to staff roles, including two who later led their own product teams.” |
| “With his assistance, the program rose 30 places in the rankings.” | “With his assistance, the checkout team launched a new payment method that became the leading choice among new customers in the market.” |
| “He assumed responsibility for the entire unit after serving in an analyst role.” | “After joining as a technical lead, she took responsibility for platform architecture and release operations across the engineering organization.” |
| “He served on the staff throughout the team's most successful four-year period.” | “He served as platform lead during the company's expansion from four products to 14, building tools used throughout the portfolio.” |

**Keep all three levels of credit available:** the individual who directly built a system; the leader who directed it; and the supporting specialist whose contribution helped the larger unit succeed. Athletics routinely recognizes all three. Technical precision explains the contribution without making the bio stingy about team achievement.

### Avoid importing the wrong kind of startup language

- “Founder mindset,” “10x,” “disruptor,” and “visionary” are weaker than an account of a specific launch, operation, or result.
- Calling something “zero-to-one” does not tell a reader what existed at the end; **name what shipped**.
- Do not attach product-market fit, ARR, or conversion to projects where those measures were never relevant.
- Do not mistake an internal performance narrative for public-facing prose. The staff-bio voice remains chronological, assured, and legible to outsiders.
- Do not reduce broad-scoped leadership to coordination. Say who made the decisions, controlled the work, or set the technical direction.

## 10. Addendum: Faris Aziz and the portable press-kit biography (22 October 2026)

**This section adds a distribution convention to the existing career-writing rules.** A strong biography can be prepared for *reuse by other people*. The press kit is not the career record itself; it is a way to let organizers, colleagues, hosts, and publishers accurately repeat that record.

### The example: Faris Aziz

[Faris Aziz's press kit](https://www.faziz-dev.com/press-kit) offers third-person bios at **55, 143, and 272 words**, along with pronunciation, a professional title line, location, links, images in various crops, and a speaker rider. He explicitly permits event organizers to use the materials without requesting approval each time.

The biographies hold the same career together at three depths:

| Length | What Faris gives the reader | Writing rule to borrow |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Short — 55 words** | Present standing as Staff Frontend Engineer; technical specialties; movement from founding engineer through leadership to Staff; systems used by tens of millions; ZurichJS and speaking. | **Identify the person, the ladder, the scale, and one achievement outside the day job.** |
| **Medium — 143 words** | Adds different company stages and industries, commercially meaningful reach, a recognized community role, and recurring topics of technical teaching. | **Add responsibility and range without changing the professional identity.** |
| **Long — 272 words** | Adds specific work: greenfield products, modernization of legacy systems, European team leadership, engineering career ladders, reliability, and the substance of talks. | **Explain what the person has actually done, not just why they're interesting.** |

His separate [track record](https://www.faziz-dev.com/impact) supplies the figures behind the bio: payment methods introduced, subscription changes, performance improvements, release-process improvements, product work, the chronology of jobs, and delivered speaking engagements. Specific measures are defined so that a reader knows what each number represents. Speaking and community activity have their own counts rather than being presented as substitutes for technical standing.

His [services page](https://www.faziz-dev.com/services) is a *different document*: it explains when someone might engage him for speaking, advisory work, or mentorship. This separation prevents the biography from reading like a sales pitch. The record establishes a professional; an offerings page explains a possible engagement.

### A biography should travel intact

A useful external introduction should not depend on what the host already knows about the practitioner. Make the copy portable:

1. **Same underlying facts.** Role, career progression, specialties, signature work, reach, and independent achievements must agree across lengths and platforms.
2. **Different amounts of detail.** A short version selects; it doesn't diminish the person's earlier authority. A long version elaborates; it doesn't invent a different career identity.
3. **Readiness for third-party reuse.** Use third person, publish a recognizable title, provide the name and its pronunciation where helpful, and supply copy people can paste with minimal editing.
4. **Career record behind the introduction.** Readers who need more than the short bio can follow a link to a substantive record of products, teams, appointments, and results.
5. **Public work remains visible.** Talks, interviews, publications, and communities can have their own credit and chronology. They do not displace executive, engineering, or product accomplishments.

This is a *writing and attribution* convention, not an instruction about page structure or visual design.

### Three lengths: how to expand rather than inflate

| Element | Short / program listing | Medium / speaker or professional introduction | Long / press or full professional profile |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Current professional standing | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Technical/professional specialty | Brief | Specific | With representative work |
| Earlier leadership and progression | One compact phrase | Named appointments or stages | Multiple stops with accomplishments |
| Scale and outcome | One memorable fact | Several facts when meaningful | Scope, decisions, results, and continuity |
| Independent contributions | One representative fact | Speaking/community/publications | Established record of each |
| Methods or philosophy | Usually omit | Only if useful | Only after the performance record |

The three bios must be consistent enough that someone introduced with the shortest version can read the longer one without discovering a different professional. An event host should never have to infer whether the practitioner has led teams, shipped software, or advised executives from a list of conference topics.

### Fictional demonstration: one career, three lengths

*The person and all achievements below are fictional, continuing the Ari Patel example from Section 5. They illustrate expansion, not new biographical claims about anyone.*

**Short — event introduction**

> Ari Patel is a principal product engineer and former vice president of engineering who has led software teams and shipped products for more than 15 years. She previously directed a 60-person engineering organization, helped launch three products, and established shared release and component systems. She continues to build software and support engineering leaders.

**Medium — speaker or staff introduction**

> Ari Patel is a principal product engineer at Vesper, bringing more than 15 years of software development and engineering leadership experience. Before returning to hands-on product work, she served as vice president of engineering at Meridian, directing a 60-person organization responsible for its web products, platform services, and developer experience. During her tenure, the organization launched three products and introduced a shared design-and-code component library. Earlier at Tern, Patel led the transition from one mobile application to a broader portfolio and established a common release process. Four engineers she managed later became engineering managers. Patel continues to contribute code, prototypes, and technical writing.

**Long — expand the existing full record, don't rewrite it**

> Ari Patel joined Vesper in 2026 as a principal product engineer after a career spanning product development, technical leadership, and engineering management. Her work covers product architecture, web software, developer experience, and the practices teams use to deliver working systems.
>
> Previously, Patel was vice president of engineering at Meridian, where she directed a 60-person organization overseeing web applications, platform services, and developer tooling. During her tenure, her teams launched three new products, consolidated engineering work following an acquisition, and introduced a shared design-and-code component library. The library provided a common foundation across the company's product lines.
>
> At Tern, Patel served as engineering director and led the mobile organization's transition from a single application to a portfolio of products. She established the mobile team's first shared release process and managed engineering staff during the transition. Four engineers who worked under her leadership subsequently moved into management roles.
>
> Patel began her career as a frontend engineer. She continues to work directly on product code and prototypes alongside technical writing and leadership responsibilities.

Observe the construction: short **recognition**, medium **responsibility and results**, long **an accumulated professional record**. Each keeps the person's prior authority intact. Each can be read aloud by somebody who has never met them.

### A press-kit narrative checklist (not an audit)

For each length, ask whether a reader can identify the person's **current standing, previous level of responsibility, recognizable accomplishments, and at least one specific result**. For use by others, also provide a consistent preferred title, pronunciation where needed, and links to the deeper record. Do not force all speaking, product, leadership, or community facts into every version.

## 12. Addendum: the first-person developer and independent-builder register (22 October 2026)

**This section extends Sections 1–11.** The Kentucky register remains the standard for generous career credit; the technical-company and SaaS sections remain the standard for describing scope, decisions, shipped work, and adoption. This addition addresses a different writing situation: an established practitioner introducing their *current practice* in the first person, particularly when that practice combines software, design, research, publishing, teaching, and community-building.

It is a content and narrative convention, not a visual design specification or a demand to adopt an engineering job title. It is useful for personal About pages, maker profiles, open-source contributor introductions, project biographies, and other first-person descriptions. Third-person staff biographies and portable speaker introductions still follow the existing guidance.

### What the developer biographies contribute

In a staff biography, **the principal unit is an appointment and its record**: remit, team, decisions, accomplishments, and preceding positions. In a first-person builder biography, **the principal unit is an active practice**: what someone makes, which problems they keep returning to, what they maintain or contribute to, and the communities they help sustain. The same earlier accomplishments remain available in both accounts.

Several real examples demonstrate different ways to do this:

| Practitioner | Distinctive editorial convention | What to borrow |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Lea Verou](https://verou.me/about/) | Describes an expansive practice through tools, products, standards, research, and independently recognizable accomplishments. | Give breadth substance through named implementations, roles, and results. |
| [Chris Coyier](https://chriscoyier.net/bio/) | Treats software products, publishing, and developer communities as parts of one professional practice. | Community-building can be a core output, rather than a side activity. |
| [Anthony Fu](https://antfu.me/Anthony) | Separates tools created from projects contributed to or maintained. | Use exact project-relationship verbs instead of a vague portfolio list. |
| [Maggie Appleton](https://maggieappleton.com/about) | Makes interdisciplinary interests legible without forcing them into one conventional occupation. | Interests can explain the continuity among unlike projects. |
| [Jeremy Keith](https://adactio.com/about/) | Includes sustained writing, community projects, events, and technical practice in the same account. | Long-running stewardship and convening count as work. |
| [Ahmad Shadeed](https://ishadeed.com/about/) | Describes design, coding, technical writing, and teaching through concrete outputs. | A design-oriented maker need not choose between craft and explanation. |
| [Paco Coursey](https://paco.me/) and [Emil Kowalski](https://emilkowal.ski/) | Put current craft and distinctive interface work near the center of brief introductions. | Short bios may establish a sharply defined current practice before any history. |

These are conventions to study, not titles or claims to borrow verbatim. A person who builds software need not claim to have spent their career as an engineer; a leader who returns to hands-on work need not diminish earlier responsibility.

### Five editorial moves

**1. Establish current activity before explaining the career.** Use present-tense verbs attached to real objects: *I build [tool]; I maintain [library]; I write about [specific question]; I organize [community or event].* A recent project or a durable area of practice can introduce the person more effectively than an exhaustive list of previous employers. This is a present-day snapshot, not a chronological autobiography.

**2. Use interests to connect the work, not to decorate the writer.** An interest is useful when it explains the selection of projects or the kinds of problems the person repeatedly investigates. “I am interested in how people recover from errors in public systems” is more informative when followed by a tool, research effort, or relevant decision. Avoid replacing the work with declarations of passion, personality, or philosophy. The existing guide's rule against opening with abstract beliefs remains intact.

**3. Name the relationship to every project.** *Created, co-created, designed, developed, shipped, maintains, contributes to, experiments with, formerly maintained,* and *led the team behind* make different claims. Use the one that fits. A prototype, an experimental branch, a maintained open-source package, an adopted service, and a company product need not be presented as equivalent accomplishments. Describe what is currently usable and what is still being tested without apologetic qualification.

**4. Give communities and shared resources maker-level credit.** Creating or directing a conference, founding a recurring gathering, running a publication, maintaining shared documentation, and organizing a contributor network are things someone builds and operates. Name the thing, the person's responsibility, and what it enabled. Mere membership and organizational leadership should be distinguished. This extends the Kentucky guide's treatment of operational work and the technical-company guide's recognition of capabilities that enable others.

**5. Bring earlier authority into the snapshot selectively.** A significant prior appointment, shipped product, founding role, or team outcome can explain the scale and experience behind current work. It does not need to trigger a march through every job. Preserve the full career record elsewhere and draw from it when the earlier achievement clarifies the present practice. Never rewrite a former director as a perpetual junior practitioner simply because the person now builds independent tools.

### Language for work that has several forms

| Relationship | Useful construction | What it actually claims |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Original creator | “I built…” / “I started…” | Initiated and produced a specific artifact or effort. |
| Collaborative creator | “I co-created…” / “We built…” | Shared authorship of something identifiable. |
| Product owner | “I developed and shipped…” / “I led the release of…” | Held responsibility for a functioning release and decisions around it. |
| Maintainer | “I maintain…” | Continued stewardship of an existing project or resource. |
| Contributor | “I contribute to…” | Participates in another project's work; does not imply founding or sole ownership. |
| Experimenter | “I'm experimenting with…” | Investigation, prototype, or research still in progress; not necessarily a public launch. |
| Community builder | “I founded…” / “I organize…” / “I run…” | Created or operates a continuing gathering, publication, or network. |
| Organizational leader | “I led [team] responsible for…” | Managed the people and decisions behind an identifiable result. |
| Teacher or writer | “I teach…” / “I publish…” | Produces instruction or written work; ideally name the subjects and outlets. |

The verbs are evidence-bearing. They should remain specific even in a relaxed first-person voice. Open-source credibility does not require a large commit count or a corporate title, but readers should be able to discover what the project does and what the writer's contribution was.

### An order of attention, not a compulsory outline

A first-person maker biography often works when it addresses these matters in roughly this order: **current practice; one or two named things being built or maintained; the recurring interests that connect them; selectively chosen previous work showing range and responsibility; community, teaching, and writing that extend the practice.** The proportions depend on the reader and purpose. A short developer introduction may need only the first two; a substantial About page can hold all five.

This order differs deliberately from Section 4's reverse-chronological staff biography. Both use the *same underlying record*. The first person chooses a present-tense vantage point; the third person makes the accumulated appointments and outcomes portable for someone else to repeat.

### Two versions of one fictional practice

The examples below are entirely invented. They demonstrate selection and narrative perspective, not an ideal professional identity.

**Third-person professional register:**

> Morgan Vale is an independent product designer and software builder who previously directed digital services at Harbor County. Vale led a team responsible for the county's appointment and records systems and helped launch a shared design library used across four departments. Vale also co-founded the Local Tools gathering and maintains an open-source form-testing utility.

**First-person builder register:**

> I build small tools for people who work with complicated forms and services. Right now I'm maintaining Fieldnote, an open-source form-testing utility, and experimenting with a way to make application requirements easier to inspect. I'm interested in what happens when a process breaks and someone has to work out how to continue. Earlier, I directed digital services at Harbor County, where our team launched a shared design library across four departments. I also co-founded Local Tools, a gathering for people building practical software in their communities.

The second version foregrounds a current practice, uses an interest to make the project selection intelligible, and retains meaningful earlier authority without narrating every career step. Both identify tangible work and use appropriately different forms of credit.

### Boundaries with the existing guide

- **Do not use occupational labels as a substitute for the record.** “Design engineer,” “developer,” “designer who codes,” and “builder” can be accurate where chosen, but projects and responsibilities should give those labels substance.
- **Do not treat personal projects as hobbies by default.** Independent tools, experiments, publishing, and community work may be central to the practice even when they are unpaid or early-stage.
- **Do not inflate projects into launches or communities into movements.** State what exists, what is maintained, and who uses or participates in it when known.
- **Do not make technical work the only legitimate accomplishment.** Product judgment, implementation, maintenance, standards, mentoring, publication, and convening may all produce durable outcomes.
- **Do not erase the managerial record.** A current maker's biography can retain the scope of teams, budgets, platforms, or programs previously directed.
- **Do not convert an About biography into a Show HN pitch.** A Show HN submission should explain a specific thing people can try; the biography introduces the person and their broader work. Both benefit from precise authorship and actual artifacts.

**Addendum rule:** The developer register supplies *first-person, present-tense authorship and ongoing stewardship* to the existing Kentucky, technical-company, SaaS, and portable-biography conventions. It changes which part of an accumulated record receives attention first; it does not change the standard for claiming the work.

## 13. The executive/operator register: describe responsibility, authority, and delivery (22 October 2026)

**This section is the practical translation tool for careers that were filed under design, IT, content, or communications but involved running programs, directing technology work, making decisions, and leading organizations.** The point is to describe the operating function a person exercised. Historical job titles remain useful context, but the record of responsibility supplies professional standing.

The writing move is simple: **make the object of the sentence the team, program, product, operating function, decision, or portfolio that the person was responsible for.** Put the method and discipline in supporting positions.

A career that crossed several professions can be introduced through a consistent level of responsibility without an elaborate story about why each career transition happened. The unit of accountability—not the oldest or newest occupational label—tells the reader what kind of operator this person has been.

### 13.1. Convert disciplines into functions

| Discipline-centered account | Executive/operator account | What a new reader understands |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Led a design team | Built and directed a multidisciplinary delivery organization | Management scope and organizational authority |
| Introduced a new design practice | Established a new organizational function and its operating capabilities | A first, a new capability, and responsibility for making it work |
| Worked on user experience | Led modernization of a service, application, or digital product | Program and product outcomes |
| Facilitated difficult meetings | Resolved competing priorities and drove decisions into execution | Decision-making and operational intervention |
| Aligned stakeholders | Secured agreement and commitments among teams responsible for delivery | Mobilization across boundaries |
| Helped teams launch things | Directed initiatives from concept through implementation | Execution and delivery ownership |
| Supported executive leadership | Carried out strategic assignments for senior executives | Mandate and executive judgment |
| Worked in content strategy | Directed editorial operations and external communications | Communications leadership |
| Helped redesign a website | Led digital modernization and repositioned an organization's public presence | Technical and communications scope |
| Built a small technical prototype | Developed, shipped, and operated a working product | Technical authorship, release, and stewardship |
| Mentored colleagues | Developed talent and established leadership capacity | Organizational succession and scaling |
| Organized community events | Founded and operated programs, assembled partners, and brought initiatives into service | Founding and program management |

These are changes in **what the sentence takes responsibility for**. “Worked on” usually introduces a method or activity. “Directed” plus a named program or organization establishes a remit. The verb earns its force through its object and consequence.

### 13.2. The operating verb families

| Responsibility | Verbs | Useful objects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Direction and decisions** | Determined, set, prioritized, selected, commissioned, approved, authorized, governed, reset | Strategy, priorities, standards, portfolio, roadmap, publication |
| **Running an organization** | Directed, oversaw, managed, operated, staffed, owned, chaired, allocated | Team, unit, budget, function, delivery operation, portfolio |
| **Founding and implementation** | Originated, founded, established, stood up, built, implemented, launched, deployed, shipped | Program, capability, practice, service, product, platform |
| **Recovery and intervention** | Diagnosed, intervened, resolved, unblocked, stabilized, recovered, reorganized, redesigned | Failed process, stalled initiative, delivery problem, decision bottleneck |
| **Coalition and executive work** | Advised, negotiated, secured, recruited, mobilized, coordinated, brokered | Executive decisions, commitments, resources, partners, leadership teams |
| **Expansion and continuity** | Hired, developed, promoted, scaled, expanded, trained, delegated, transitioned, institutionalized | People, teams, adoption, practices, successor leadership |
| **Technical and specialist judgment** | Architected, engineered, evaluated, modeled, assessed, investigated, maintained | Software, data systems, technology options, procurement, implementations |
| **Editorial authority** | Directed, commissioned, wrote, edited, approved, published, repositioned | Communications operation, publishing program, public narrative, portfolio report |

*Owned*, *directed*, *advised*, *established*, and *contributed* describe different roles in work that is often collective. These distinctions make the account clear without reducing it to an attribution exercise. The Kentucky convention generously credits a leader with the achievements of the unit they served or led; the technical convention names what the practitioner personally built, owned, or shipped.

### 13.3. The transcription table: from how the work felt to what the work was

These examples translate informal, lived descriptions into language recognized in executive, technology, and program-delivery environments. They are illustrative sentence constructions, **not a prescribed biography**.

| What someone might say | Professional language |
| --- | --- |
| “I unfucked a lot of meetings.” | Resolved organizational bottlenecks, established clearer decision processes, and moved stalled work into execution. |
| “I took the time to understand people and got them moving.” | Built the relationships and commitments needed to execute work across organizational boundaries. |
| “I could get people to do things nobody else could.” | Mobilized cross-functional teams and secured commitments to deliver difficult initiatives. |
| “They brought me into complicated situations.” | Took responsibility for high-priority strategic assignments requiring independent operational judgment. |
| “I knew the rules and how to get around obstacles.” | Navigated operating constraints and developed workable paths to implementation. |
| “I made the call and got things going.” | Set direction, made trade-offs, assigned responsibilities, and directed execution. |
| “I came up with something, found good people, and let them run it.” | Originated a program, assembled its founding team, established its operating model, and transitioned ownership to new leadership. |
| “I hired people and helped them find their footing.” | Recruited and developed the team, established new capabilities, and built internal leadership capacity. |
| “I ran communications in addition to my other responsibilities.” | Held concurrent leadership responsibility for organizational communications, editorial strategy, and publishing operations. |
| “I approved every post.” | Held final editorial authority across the organization's public publishing program. |
| “I researched and counted everything the organization had ever delivered.” | Directed a historical portfolio review and classification effort for executive and external reporting. |
| “I was chief of staff for a parent office with an arcane org chart.” | Held an executive operations assignment serving leadership across a multi-program technology portfolio. |
| “Agency leaders called when they needed someone to fix things.” | Undertook senior advisory and strategic-delivery assignments for agency executives. |
| “I made a tool because I wanted it to exist.” | Built and deployed an independent product addressing a recurring technical or operational problem. |
| “I helped people learn to do ambitious work.” | Developed professional capability, cultivated emerging leaders, and established practices others could carry forward. |

The useful sentence is **verb + responsibility + effect**: “Directed [what] across [which remit], producing [what change].”

An executive introduction can name the responsibility first. The fuller Kentucky biography can carry the person's tenure, the unit's achievements, and their record across successive appointments.

### 13.4. The career-level descriptor is separate from the employment title

A job title records how an employer classified a position. A career-level descriptor says what sort of work someone has been entrusted to do across appointments.

Technology and delivery leadership often occurs in jobs coded as **IT, communications, design, content, program management, and special initiatives**. The professional identity can reflect that collective responsibility directly. The historical titles remain available in the career record.

| Useful professional descriptor | Responsibilities it can cover |
| --- | --- |
| **Technology and delivery executive** | Organizational leadership, technology strategy, program execution, implementation, delivery outcomes |
| **Strategy and operations leader** | Executive priorities, decisions, operating models, cross-team execution |
| **Program and portfolio leader** | Initiating, operating, recovering, and expanding programs across teams or organizations |
| **Product and technology leader** | Deciding what to build, directing technical delivery, launching and running products |
| **Communications and editorial executive** | Publication authority, messaging, public-facing systems, editorial operations |
| **Founder and independent builder** | Creating products, events, programs, and operating structures from scratch |

These descriptors are **ways to introduce a cumulative professional record**, not replacements for the actual titles used by organizations. A broad career introduction can lead with a function that has been exercised repeatedly and reserve the detailed promotion history for the long account.

### 13.5. Concurrent and less-visible responsibilities deserve their own sentences

An appointment may cover several operating functions: an organization led, a communications channel directed, a digital product modernized, and an executive initiative carried through delivery. All belong in the record even when one formal title sat above them.

**Generic example:**

> Directed a multidisciplinary delivery function and established a specialist capability. Concurrently oversaw editorial operations and the modernization of the organization's public-facing platform. Later served in an executive operations assignment spanning a broader technology portfolio.

The example is deliberately about the functions and their progression in scope. The job history can carry the formal titles, exact dates, and reporting relationships elsewhere. The short introduction should make the level of responsibility understandable before anyone needs an organizational chart.

### 13.6. Claim the scale of the responsibility, including collective results

Kentucky biographies connect assistants and specialists to the achievements of the teams they served. Executive and board biographies similarly communicate the scale of functions, programs, people, and resources associated with a person's leadership.

| Kind of responsibility | Meaningful measure of scale |
| --- | --- |
| Led a function | Staff and managers directed; teams supported; specialties founded; organization served |
| Ran a delivery portfolio | Programs, products, clients, partner organizations, complexity, operational reach |
| Directed communications | Editorial control; channels; publication volume; public audience; organizational coverage |
| Led executive reporting | Portfolio size, time span, classifications produced, organizational decisions enabled |
| Founded an initiative | Participants and collaborators mobilized; programs or products launched; work continued |
| Built software | Technical scope, capabilities shipped, adoption, repeat use, maintenance obligations |
| Advised senior leadership | Breadth of assignments, type of judgment, level of decision-maker, organizations involved |
| Developed people | Hiring, promotion, professional development, new leaders, successor teams |

Use **“under her direction,” “his team delivered,” “with his assistance,” “during her tenure,”** and **“was instrumental in”** where those constructions illuminate an authentic contribution to team accomplishments. This retains the generosity of the Kentucky register.

### 13.7. Apply the same record at different lengths and for different audiences

The portable-bio lesson from Faris Aziz becomes more powerful once the professional account is written at the right level.

- **Short introduction:** Professional standing + one recognizable kind of responsibility + a consequential example or measure.
- **Medium biography:** Current work, previous authority, and a small number of achievements from distinct phases of the career.
- **Long biography:** Career stops, overlapping functions, products and programs directed, people developed, team successes, public standing, and earlier specialties.
- **Assignment page:** Mandate, decisions taken, team or system led, implementation, and resulting capability.
- **First-person About page:** What the person builds or directs now, with accumulated executive and technical history nearby.

An introduction for a technology conference may give product and technical work more prominence. An introduction for a board, mayor, or institutional executive may name program direction and operating authority first. The underlying work stays consistent; the choice of what to foreground changes.

### 13.8. Six steps to rewrite a raw account

1. **Identify the unit of responsibility.** Team, product, program, portfolio, communications operation, decision, or organizational function.
2. **Name the authority exercised.** Direct control, independent execution, formal management, founding responsibility, or executive advisory.
3. **Choose an operating verb.** Directed, established, launched, resolved, scaled, advised, engineered, transitioned.
4. **Name the change.** A product went live, a program became operational, a decision was reached, a process improved, or a team gained capability.
5. **Attach meaningful scope.** People, programs, products, audiences, organizations, years, geography, adoption, or output.
6. **Supply employer and title context when it helps.** The institution explains the setting; the responsibility explains the work.

**Fictional worked example**

Raw: “I kept getting called into weekly product meetings because nobody could make a decision, and I got it moving.”

Operating register: “Brought in to resolve stalled product delivery, Morgan clarified decision rights across engineering and product leadership, reset the release plan, and directed the next launch.”

Kentucky continuation: “During Morgan's tenure, the team introduced two new services and developed three engineers who later moved into leadership roles.”

Startup continuation: “The release reached production, and the team expanded adoption across its customer base.”

The continuation changes with the audience. The account of the person's responsibility does not.

### 13.9. Rules to carry back into the rest of the guide

**Lead with the function exercised.** The type of responsibility matters more than the specialized method.

**Use authority-bearing verbs.** Led, built, directed, owned, established, implemented, resolved, launched, developed, and scaled each make a different kind of professional contribution legible.

**Give concurrent responsibilities room.** The communications director, program leader, product builder, and executive operator can all be the same person at different moments or simultaneously.

**Preserve the early record.** A person who directed products, technology, and communications before joining a prominent institution enters that institution with an already substantial record.

**Use collective credit confidently.** The person's unit, team, organization, and program accomplishments form part of their professional history.

**Keep the biography portable.** The short version conveys standing and memorable scope. The longer version contains the many assignments that demonstrate it.

**Core rule:** The discipline explains *how* someone worked. The executive/operator register explains *what they were trusted to direct, decide, build, change, and deliver*.
